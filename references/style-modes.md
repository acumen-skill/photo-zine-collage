# Style modes

Load only the mode chosen by the user. Keep the photograph itself photographic in both modes.

## Impressionist blocks

Use `impressionist-blocks` for an energetic painterly field that reads in clear color masses without sacrificing all mid-scale detail.

### Visual construction

- Build eight to fourteen interlocking medium-to-large oil-painted planes. Keep any one plane below roughly one quarter of the painted field.
- Let shapes follow the source geometry: a hillside becomes a sloping plane, a canopy becomes a broken mass, and water becomes horizontal color bands.
- Use visible bristle direction and occasional dry-brush gaps. Keep edges soft or broken, not airbrushed.
- Preserve four to six mid-scale cues that make the particular scene recognizable: a path bend, trunk group, roof edge, window rhythm, shoreline, figure gesture, or similar structure.
- Add a sparse layer of smaller bristle accents and broken contours across about 5–10% of the painted field. These details should reward normal-size viewing without turning into all-over texture.
- Let one or two painted strokes cross the paper boundary and visually stitch the photo to the extension.

### Color behavior

- Reuse the extracted palette at comparable lightness and saturation.
- Mix adjacent source colors optically through broken strokes rather than adding a new contrast color.
- Keep the most saturated extracted color to a small supporting area when it is minor in the source.
- Do not cover the composition with a cream, sepia, gray, or pastel veil.

### Avoid

Avoid both extremes: no featureless mega-blocks that erase scene structure, and no tiny repeated marks or pointillist noise. Also avoid thick black outlines, palette-knife impasto that obscures the scene, psychedelic contrast, muddy overmixing, or generic decorative paint swatches.

## Realist fine lines

Use `realist-fine-lines` for a quieter extension with accurate structure and elegant detail.

### Visual construction

- Keep perspective, proportions, subject direction, and the source light consistent.
- Extend forms with thin tapered strokes, restrained cross-hatching, short contour fragments, and small controlled brush marks.
- Vary line weight gently: slightly firmer near the focal transition and lighter toward open paper.
- Describe foliage, fabric, hair, and grass with selected clusters rather than drawing every strand or leaf.
- Keep the overall field airy. Detail should reward close viewing without becoming a dense engraving.
- Carry at least two real edges or rhythms through the paper boundary so the collage feels joined rather than framed.

### Color behavior

- Match local source colors before adding detail.
- Use thin translucent paint only where the source is light; keep dark source regions appropriately dark.
- Derive neutrals by mixing palette colors. Do not introduce a cold gray cast or warm vintage tint across the image.
- Keep skin, sky, vegetation, and product colors natural and source-specific.

### Avoid

Avoid coarse comic inking, uniform outlines, hyper-sharp digital rendering, photorealistic duplication of every background detail, pale washed vegetation, generic beige paper grading, or ornamental botanical filigree.

## Shared seam treatment

In either mode:

1. Align at least two generated forms with visible source edges.
2. Match lighting direction and local contrast on both sides of the join.
3. Keep a narrow irregular paper edge visible across part of the fragment.
4. Allow a few painted marks to pass over or under that edge.
5. Leave one section of the boundary quiet so the material change remains readable.

The seam should alternate between continuation and interruption. A perfectly hidden seam looks like a normal image edit; a fully outlined seam looks like a sticker.
